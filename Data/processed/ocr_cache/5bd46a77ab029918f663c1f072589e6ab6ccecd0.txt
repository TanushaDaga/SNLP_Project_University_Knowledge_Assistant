Jobocalypse Bogey
Reports of AI eating our jobs have been exaggerated.
Long-term, it's likely to create more jobs than it destroys
n Oct 2016, an Uber subsidiary called Otto delivered 52,000 cans
of beer to a warehouse 200km away, on a self-driving truck. It
rapid scale-up, because autonomous trucks don't need breaks, and
save on driver wages, which make up a third of freight costs. But 10
years later, that revolution is still in the future. Another revolution
that we've been warned of repeatedly since ChatGPT's rollout in
2022-AIeating ourjobs-isn'tquite happening to schedule.OpenA1
CEO Sam Altman had said Al could eliminate up to half of all
existingjobs. Rival Dario Amodei narrowed his warning to half of
entry-level roles, while Elon Musk, characteristically, promised
that work itself would become history.
So, where are we four years on? In US, whichleads the world in Al
development and deployment, the job market shows no sign of the
dreaded job apocalypse. Last month, US actuaily added 1.6L jobs, a
healthy number, and the unemployment
rateremained unchanged. Infact, employer
surveys show fewer firms now expect
Al-related job cuts than was the case
last year: The Economist points out that
Al has actually caused a net increase
in US jobs since 2023 - 1mn jobs created,
against 2L erased. So, is thejob apocalypse
a myth? While it's not the majority view,
sceptics think it is. They say AI firms
can justify their colossal valuations
and investments only by painting a future in which AI slashes
the wage bill to zero.
A more reasonable view is that Al, like any disruptive tech, will
destroy many jobs, but its net impact on the job market will be
positive. This has happened throughout human history. For example,
60% of jobs in 2018 didn't exist before:1940. In 1930s, Keymes worried
about “technological unemployment"- advances “"outrunning the
pace at which we can find new uses for labour, but'global wealth
and well-being have growm manifold over the past century. AI will
also likely play the same role. Instead of completely taking over
jobs, it will make work easier. Greater employee efficiency will
lower costs, which will bring latent demand into the market,
increasing employment. Economists call this Jevons Paradox, and
we've already seen it happen with radiologists in the past 10 years.
In 2016, AI guru Geoffrey Hinton said people should stop training
to be radiologists, because AI would do the job better. Instead,
Al-assisted radiologists are busier than ever.
